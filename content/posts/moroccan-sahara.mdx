The Sahara does not announce itself. It creeps up on you over hours of driving, the scrub thinning, the earth paling, until suddenly there is nothing but sand and sky and the sound of your own breathing. I went to Erg Chigaga — the harder-to-reach, wilder cousin of Merzouga's Erg Chebbi — for three nights. I came back quieter.

## Getting to the Edge of Nothing

Erg Chigaga sits at the end of a 60-kilometer track of piste — unmarked desert road — southwest of M'Hamid El Ghizlane, itself the end of the paved road from Marrakech. You cannot reach the dunes in a normal car. You hire a 4x4 and a driver, or you walk, or — as I did — you ride a camel for the final stretch.

### The Last Town

M'Hamid is where the road gives up. It is a cluster of mud-brick houses and date palms where the Draa River dies into the sand. I met my guide, Hassan, at a café that served mint tea so sweet it made my teeth ache. He spoke five languages and switched between them mid-sentence.

"Tomorrow, the desert," he said, pouring the tea from a height that produced a foam like a cappuccino. "Tonight, you sleep in a real bed. Take it."

## Into the Dunes

We left at dawn. The camel — mine was named Barack, after the American president, for reasons Hassan refused to explain — swayed beneath me with a rhythm that was initially terrifying and eventually hypnotic.

### The Landscape Unwinding

For the first hour the terrain was flat and stony, the hammada — desert pavement. Then the dunes began, small at first, then enormous, some over 100 meters tall, their windward sides ridged like the spine of a sleeping animal. The sand was the color of weak tea in the morning and turned to gold, then copper, then a deep burgundy as the sun fell.

## The Camp

Our camp was a small cluster of Berber tents — goat-hair canvas, woven in bands of black and brown — pitched in a hollow between two great dunes. There were no other camps visible in any direction. No light, no road, no sound but the wind.

### Mint Tea, Three Times

Hassan made tea the moment we arrived, and three times a day after. The Moroccan tea ceremony is its own grammar: the first glass is "as gentle as life," the second "as strong as love," the third "as bitter as death." I am not sure I tasted the difference, but the ritual — the long pour, the foam, the small glasses — became the rhythm of the days.

## The Night Sky

This is why you come. After sunset the sky does not darken so much as deepen, layer by layer, until the Milky Way stood across the zenith like a smear of spilled milk. I have seen dark skies before — in the Atacama, in the Himalaya — but the Sahara's was the most complete. There were so many stars that the constellations vanished into the general brightness.

> The desert is the one place left where you can see the universe whole and know, finally, how small your problems are.

Hassan played a drum made from a gas canister and sang Berber songs in a reedy tenor that the dunes swallowed and returned. I lay on the sand and watched satellites cross the sky and did not speak for a long time.

## A Practical Desert Guide

```text
# Erg Chigaga Desert Camp — What to Pack
# Temperatures swing wildly: 35°C day, 5°C night in winter.

# Clothing
- Loose, light layers (linen/cotton) for daytime
- Warm fleece + beanie for night (it gets cold)
- A scarf/shemagh — sun, wind, and sand protection
- Closed shoes you don't mind getting sandy

# Gear
- Headlamp (red light mode preserves night vision)
- Wet wipes (no showers in wild camps)
- Sunscreen + lip balm
- 3L water minimum per day
- A small daypack

# Etiquette
- Always accept the first glass of tea (refusing is rude)
- Ask before photographing people
- Dress modestly, especially in villages
- Leave no trace — pack out everything
```

## The Walk Back

On the third morning we walked out. Hassan pointed to tracks in the sand — a beetle, a jerboa, the drag-mark of a snake. "The desert is full of life," he said. "It just knows how to hide."

I did not believe him until I saw a fennec fox, ears enormous, watching us from behind a clump of broom. It held my gaze for a long moment, then vanished into the heat shimmer, and we kept walking, toward the thin green line of the oasis, and the road, and the world of noise I had, briefly, forgotten.
