Bangkok does not ask you to love it. It drops you into 35-degree heat and a wall of exhaust and chili smoke, and it lets you decide. I decided, somewhere between my third plate of pad kra pao and a mango sticky rice eaten standing on a sidewalk, that Bangkok is the greatest street food city on earth. Here is how to eat 48 hours of it.

## Morning: The River and the Wok

### 7:00 a.m. — Chao Phraya Breakfast

Start at the Tha Tien pier, where longtail boats fold the brown river into wakes. Buy a Styrofoam container of jok (rice congee) from a stall near the pier — silken, peppery, scattered with ginger and fried garlic. Eat it on a bench watching the ferries cross.

### 9:00 a.m. — Or Tor Kor Market

Take a taxi north to Or Tor Kor, the market where Bangkok's best cooks shop. It is cleaner and calmer than the famous Chatuchak weekend market, and the produce is pornographic: dragonfruit the size of footballs, mounds of green papaya, durian stacked like artillery shells. Sample what's offered. Buy a bag of rambutan for later.

## Midday: The Chinatown Maze

### Noon — Yaowarat, But Go Deep

Tourists clog Yaowarat's main drag at night. For lunch, slip into the sois (lanes) behind it. Find a vendor with a wok blackened from decades of use and order kuay teow kua gai — wide rice noodles charred with chicken and egg until the edges crackle. The best one I found was run by a woman who did not look up once during the entire cook.

### 2:00 p.m. — A Boat Noodle Pilgrimage

Take a longtail to the canal-side cluster at Wat Sai. Boat noodles — kuay teow reua — are tiny bowls of dark, intense broth (historically enriched with pork blood) served from boats that now mostly sit on land. You eat them four or five at a time, each one gone in two slurps. They cost about 50 baht ($1.40) a bowl.

## Evening: The Night Market

### 6:00 p.m. — Jodd Fairs

Jodd Fairs is the night market that locals actually use. It sprawls under string lights, smoke billowing from grills. Here is what to eat, in order:

1. **Pad kra pao** — holy basil stir-fried with pork and a fried egg over rice. The national dish, eaten daily, never the same twice.
2. **Moo ping** — skewers of grilled pork, slightly sweet, charred at the tips.
3. **Khanom jeen nam ya** — fermented rice noodles in a curry fish sauce that sounds wrong and tastes right.

### 9:00 p.m. — The Dessert

End at a mango sticky rice cart. The rice is coconut-steeped, the mango impossibly ripe, the coconut cream poured on top still warm. It is the perfect end to a day that was, fundamentally, about chili.

### The Rules of Eating Well

- **Follow the line.** A queue of locals means the food is worth the wait.
- **Eat where there's no English menu.** Point. Smile. You'll get something good.
- **Watch the turnover.** High volume means fresh ingredients.
- **Carry hand sanitizer and small bills.** 100-baht notes break the rhythm.

## The Soundtrack of the City

What I remember most is not any single dish but the sound: the percussion of woks, the hiss of batter hitting oil, the vendor calling prices in a singsong that never stopped. Bangkok feeds you whether you asked to be fed or not. You simply turn a corner, and there it is — a cart, a flame, a woman who has made this one thing, perfectly, for twenty years.

> A city's food is its autobiography, written in grease and chili and steam.

Two days is enough to fall in. It is not enough to finish. Bangkok, like all great food cities, saves its best secrets for the third visit, the tenth, the twentieth. Go hungry. Go often.
