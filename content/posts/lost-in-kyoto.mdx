There is a particular silence that lives inside Kyoto. It is not the silence of emptiness, but the kind that holds centuries of incense smoke and chanting and rain on moss. I came for the cherry blossoms; I stayed for that silence.

## Arriving in the Old Capital

I landed at Kansai Airport on a grey afternoon in early April, the haru (spring) still trembling on the edge of full bloom. The train into Kyoto wound past suburban sprawl that looked unremarkable — until the mountains rose suddenly in the distance, blue and patient, and I felt the city announce itself not with skyscrapers but with restraint.

My ryokan sat on a narrow lane in Gion, behind a wooden gate marked only by a small cloth noren curtain. The owner, a woman in her sixties named Tanaka-san, bowed when I arrived and said very little else for the entire week. This, I would learn, is the Kyoto way.

### The Art of Doing Less

On my first full day I committed to a single destination: Ryōan-ji, the temple of the famous rock garden. I sat on the veranda for two hours. Fifteen rocks arranged in white gravel, arranged so that from any angle one is always hidden. The garden is a lesson in incompletion, and I had traveled six thousand miles to learn it.

## Temples and Tea

The days that followed took on a gentle rhythm. Mornings began early, before the tour buses, when the temples belonged to the sweepers and the cats.

### Kinkaku-ji at Dawn

The Golden Pavilion floats on its pond like a dream too bright to be real. I arrived at 7:00 a.m., just after opening, and for ten perfect minutes I had it nearly to myself. The gold leaf caught the morning sun and threw it back in fractured pieces across the water.

### A Matcha Ceremony in Higashiyama

In a teahouse off Sannenzaka, a tea master named Hiroko guided me through a chakai — a informal tea gathering. She whisked the matcha until a fine jade foam crowned the bowl, then turned it twice before placing it in my hands.

"Drink," she said, "and taste the season."

It tasted of green, of April, of something vegetal and bright that I have never quite found again.

## Cherry Blossoms on the Philosopher's Path

On the sixth day, the sakura finally reached full bloom. I walked the Philosopher's Path — a stone canal trail lined with hundreds of cherry trees — and the petals fell like quiet snow, settling on the water and on my shoulders and on the shoulders of strangers who all, somehow, were smiling.

> We do not travel to arrive. We travel to be changed by the arriving.

That evening I sat by the Kamogawa River and watched couples and students and old men with their dogs, all of them moving at the speed of the season. No one was in a hurry. The blossoms would be gone in a week.

## A Note on Slowness

Kyoto taught me that a city is not its checklist. I saw perhaps a dozen temples in seven days, and I remember them not as line items but as textures — the grain of a wooden floor, the cold of a stone lantern, the green of moss so thick it looked painted.

If you go, go slowly. Sit on a veranda. Drink the tea. Let one temple be enough for a morning. The silence will hold you, and you will leave more yourself than when you arrived.
